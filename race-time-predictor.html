What this calculator does

If you know how fast you ran one distance, you can make a reasonable guess at how fast you could run another. This predictor takes one recent run and estimates your time for a 5K, 10K, half marathon and marathon. It is meant to help you choose a realistic goal, not to promise a result.

How it is calculated

New time = your time × (new distance ÷ your distance) ^ 1.06

This is the Riegel formula, published by Pete Riegel in 1981. The 1.06 exponent means that you slow down slightly, per kilometre, as the distance grows. The calculator applies the formula to each race distance and then divides by the distance to show the pace you would need.

Worked example

A runner completes a 5K in 25:00 (1,500 seconds). For a 10K the distance ratio is 10 ÷ 5 = 2, and 2 ^ 1.06 ≈ 2.085, so the estimate is 1,500 × 2.085 ≈ 3,127 seconds, or about 52:07 (5:13 per km). The same steps give about 1:55:00 for a half marathon and about 3:59:47 for a marathon. The marathon figure is the least reliable of these, because it needs far more endurance training than a 5K does. Press "Try an example" above to see the full table.

Limitations

  • It works best when the distance you enter is a hard, recent effort and the target distance is not very different from it.
  • Predictions for much longer races tend to be optimistic unless your training includes long runs.
  • Heat, hills, altitude, sleep, nutrition and pacing mistakes are not included.
  • It was built from running results and does not suit walking or cycling.
  • Inputs under 1.5 km or over 50 km, or a pace slower than 20:00 or faster than 2:30 per km, are rejected as probable typing mistakes.

Common questions

Which recent run should I enter?

Use a recent race or a hard, steady effort on a fairly flat route, ideally between 5 km and 15 km. An easy training jog will make the predictions too slow.

Can I predict my marathon time from a 5K?

The calculator will give a number, but it is usually optimistic. A marathon needs long-run training and fuelling that a fast 5K does not prove. Treat it as an upper limit of what is possible, not a promise.

How accurate are the predictions?

They are most useful when the new distance is not far from the one you entered and you have trained for it. Heat, hills, sleep, nutrition and pacing can move a real result by several minutes.

Does this work for cycling or walking?

No. The formula was developed from running results. Cycling times depend heavily on hills, wind and drafting, so a running formula does not fit.
